Dyslexia in children: how to help at home

Why reading can be such a battle for a bright child, what actually helps, and how to tell when it's worth getting support.

The Lodestone Education Team A collaborative effort · Updated 28 July 2026 · 9 min read

If you've watched your clever, curious child struggle with a page that other children breeze through, you already know how helpless it can feel. Many parents quietly wonder whether they did something wrong, or lie awake worrying about where this leads. You're not alone in that, and it isn't your fault. There is also a great deal you can do, starting tonight.

What dyslexia actually is

Dyslexia is a language-based learning difference that makes accurate, fluent reading and spelling harder. The reason is specific: the brain processes the sounds inside words differently. It has nothing to do with intelligence, effort or eyesight, and it is not caused by too little reading at home.

Around one in ten people is dyslexic. Taught in the right way, these children absolutely can learn to read well. The earlier that teaching starts, the shorter the road tends to be.

The mythHe's lazy, or he'll grow out of it if we wait.
What's trueEffort isn't the problem. Waiting rarely helps; structured teaching does, and it works at any age.

The signs, by age

Dyslexia looks different as children grow, which is part of why it gets missed.

  • Ages 4 to 6. Trouble with rhyming, learning letter sounds, or hearing that "cat" starts with a /k/ sound. Muddling the order of sounds in longer words.
  • Ages 7 to 10. Reading slowly and with visible effort. Guessing words from the first letter or the picture. Spelling the same word three ways on one page. Avoiding reading aloud.
  • Ages 11 and up. Reading improves but stays effortful and tiring. Trouble with longer texts and note-taking. Often a marked gap between what a child can say and what they can write down.

In Malaysia there's an extra layer worth naming. Many of our children are learning to read in two or three languages at once, and a child who is struggling in BM and English may simply look "weak in languages" to a busy teacher. A reading difficulty that would be obvious in a monolingual classroom can go unnoticed here for years.

A simple check you can do at home

This is an observation guide, not a test, and it cannot diagnose anything. But it will tell you whether it's worth asking someone to look properly. Over an ordinary week, note how many of these you recognise in a child aged roughly six or older.

  • Reads far more slowly and with more effort than they speak
  • Guesses words from the first letter or the picture rather than sounding them out
  • Muddles similar letters or short words: b and d, was and saw, on and no
  • Spells the same word several different ways in one piece of writing
  • Avoids reading aloud, or is exhausted after a short read
  • Finds rhyming hard, or struggles to hear the separate sounds in a word
  • There's a visible gap between how bright they seem and how they read
  • Someone else in the family had similar trouble with reading or spelling

Several of these together don't prove dyslexia. They do mean a proper look is worthwhile, and that "wait and see" is probably the wrong advice.

What helps at home

  • Read to them every day. Share books above their own reading level. The goal is to keep the love of stories alive while the decoding is still hard.
  • Use audiobooks alongside print. Rich language builds vocabulary and confidence without the decoding battle attached.
  • Play with sounds, not just letters. Rhyming games, clapping syllables, "what sound does it start with?" This is the foundation reading is built on.
  • Try paired reading. Read the same line together out loud, at their pace, then let them try it alone.
  • Keep it short, daily and kind. Ten encouraging minutes will beat an hour that ends in tears, every time.
  • Never put them on the spot. No surprise reading aloud in front of relatives. Let them rehearse first if they want to perform.

Teaching methods you can borrow

The approach with the strongest evidence behind it is structured, systematic, multi-sensory phonics, often called Structured Literacy or an Orton-Gillingham approach. You don't need to be trained to use the principles at home.

  • Explicit, never guessed. Teach one letter-sound at a time and say it out loud. Don't assume they'll absorb it from context.
  • Multi-sensory. See it, say it, hear it, trace it. Writing letters in sand, salt or the air while saying the sound engages more of the brain at once.
  • Cumulative. Review yesterday before adding anything new. Repetition isn't a sign of failure here; it's the method.
  • In a logical order. Simple sounds, then blends, then longer words. Master each step rather than racing to keep up with the class.

It's worth knowing that phonics alone isn't a complete answer for every child. Research is clear that systematic phonics gives the biggest gains to the children who start out weakest, but many dyslexic learners also need explicit work on fluency, vocabulary and comprehension alongside it.

From our centre

"His teacher called him lazy three meetings in a row"

Aariz* came to us at nine. His mother's first words on the phone were that his teacher had called him lazy at three consecutive parent-teacher meetings. He could talk for twenty minutes about sharks, species, feeding habits, everything, but a single paragraph of his BM textbook took him ten minutes and left him near tears. She had already spent two years on extra tuition, which she said only made the evenings longer and the arguments worse.

What the screening showed was not laziness. He was guessing words from the first letter and the picture, and he could not reliably break a word into its separate sounds. The gap between his spoken vocabulary and his reading age was almost three years.

We started with sounds, not books. Explicit, one sound at a time, tracing letters while saying them, ten minutes at a stretch. His mother stopped the homework battles and read aloud to him at night instead, with no pressure to read back. At around week seven she messaged to say he had picked up a shark book by himself and read the caption aloud, unprompted.

That early shift is common, and it matters. But we're always honest with parents that the durable change takes longer. We ask families to commit to a minimum of six months, because that is the point at which the gains stop being a good week and start being how the child reads.

What to say to your child

Most parents want to reassure their child but worry about saying the wrong thing. These are the lines we come back to:

  • "Your brain reads in a different way, and we're going to find the way that fits you."
  • "Reading being hard doesn't mean you're not clever. You're one of the cleverest people I know."
  • "We're a team. We'll figure this out together, and there's no rush."

Talking to your child's school

Ask for a short meeting and describe what you see at home rather than leading with a label. Adjustments that are reasonable to request:

  • Extra time on reading tasks and in tests
  • Not being made to read aloud cold in front of the class
  • Instructions given verbally as well as in writing
  • Access to audiobooks or text-to-speech for longer texts
  • Marking that doesn't penalise spelling in non-language subjects

A useful closing question: "What can the school put in place, and can we review it together in a few weeks?" It turns a one-off conversation into an ongoing one.

Worth remembering. Many dyslexic children are strong at big-picture thinking, storytelling, spatial reasoning, problem-solving, art and building. Say those strengths out loud to your child often. Reading struggles erode confidence quietly, and this is the counterweight.
What tends to backfire. Telling a child to try harder when effort was never the problem. Surprise reading aloud. Relying on memorising whole words instead of teaching sounds. And waiting a year to see whether things improve on their own.
If you do one thing this week

Read aloud to your child for ten minutes tonight. You read, they just listen and enjoy it. No pressure to read anything back. It sounds too simple to matter, and it's the thing parents tell us changed the mood in the house first.

What the research says

Two findings are worth knowing as a parent. First, structured, systematic phonics consistently produces the strongest reading gains for children at risk of dyslexia, and the advantage is largest for the children who start out with the weakest skills. Second, reviews of early identification and intervention find that acting early meaningfully improves outcomes.

Put together, they say something hopeful: the children who look furthest behind are often the ones who gain most from the right teaching, and starting sooner genuinely changes the trajectory.

Questions parents ask us

Will my child grow out of dyslexia?

No, it's lifelong. But that's less bleak than it sounds. With the right teaching a dyslexic child can become a confident, capable reader. What changes is how they're taught, not whether they can learn.

Is this my fault?

No. Dyslexia is neurological and often runs in families. It isn't caused by parenting, screens, or not reading enough at home. What you do from here is what counts.

Can dyslexic children still do well in life?

Yes. With support, many go on to thrive academically and professionally. Dyslexic thinkers are strongly represented in design, engineering, entrepreneurship and the arts.

Do we need a formal diagnosis before getting help?

No. You can start support straight away. A screening shows where your child is strong and where the gaps are, which is usually enough to begin the right teaching.

How long before we see progress?

Confidence often shifts in the first few weeks. Real, durable change in reading takes longer, and we ask families for a minimum of six months before judging the work.
